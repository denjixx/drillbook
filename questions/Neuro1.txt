Q: A 58-year-old man develops right leg weakness after an infarct. Imaging shows the lesion is on the medial surface of the left frontal lobe, in the region that is a continuation of the precentral gyrus. Which structure is most likely involved?
A) Posterior paracentral lobule
B) Gyrus rectus
C) Precuneus
D) Anterior paracentral lobule
E) Cingulate gyrus
Answer: D
Explanation: The anterior paracentral lobule is the medial continuation of the precentral gyrus (motor) and contains the leg and foot area of the motor cortex. The posterior paracentral lobule continues the postcentral gyrus (sensory), and the precuneus lies behind it.

Q: Which of the following structures forms the lateral wall of the frontal (anterior) horn of the lateral ventricle?
A) Thalamus
B) Putamen
C) Septum pellucidum
D) Globus pallidus
E) Head of the caudate nucleus
Answer: E
Explanation: The head of the caudate nucleus bulges into and forms the lateral wall of the frontal horn. The septum pellucidum forms the medial wall, separating the two lateral ventricles.

Q: A patient has a lesion in the retrolenticular portion of the internal capsule. Which functional deficit is most likely?
A) Ipsilateral hemiparesis
B) Contralateral homonymous visual field defect
C) Contralateral weakness of the lower face
D) Contralateral hemisensory loss of the trunk only
E) Contralateral loss of hearing
Answer: B
Explanation: The retrolenticular portion lies behind the lentiform nucleus and carries the optic radiations. The sublenticular portion carries the auditory radiations, the genu carries corticobulbar fibers, and the posterior limb carries mainly corticospinal fibers.

Q: A neurosurgeon examines a patient after trauma to the posterior aspect of the midbrain. Which cranial nerve is the only one that exits the brainstem on its dorsal surface?
A) Oculomotor nerve (CN III)
B) Hypoglossal nerve (CN XII)
C) Accessory nerve (CN XI)
D) Trochlear nerve (CN IV)
E) Abducens nerve (CN VI)
Answer: D
Explanation: The trochlear nerve emerges from the posterior midbrain just caudal to the inferior colliculus. It is also the only cranial nerve whose fibers decussate before exiting, so it innervates the contralateral superior oblique.

Q: The rhomboid fossa contains a bulge, the facial colliculus. Which two structures does it overlie?
A) Facial motor nucleus and the superior salivatory nucleus
B) Abducens nucleus and the internal genu of the facial nerve
C) Hypoglossal nucleus and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus
D) Vestibular nuclei and the cochlear nuclei
E) Trochlear nucleus and the locus ceruleus
Answer: B
Explanation: The facial colliculus is formed by facial nerve fibers looping over the abducens nucleus. A lesion here produces ipsilateral lateral rectus palsy and facial weakness together.

Q: A patient with a posterior fossa tumor experiences intractable vomiting that is unresponsive to antiemetics acting on the periphery. Irritation of which structure on the floor of the fourth ventricle best explains this finding?
A) Hypoglossal trigone
B) Facial colliculus
C) Area postrema
D) Locus ceruleus
E) Vestibular area
Answer: C
Explanation: The area postrema, rostral to the obex, is a chemoreceptor trigger zone that lacks a blood–brain barrier and triggers vomiting in response to circulating emetics.

Q: The anterior commissure interconnects which of the following structures?
A) Olfactory bulbs and the middle and inferior temporal lobes
B) Both occipital lobes
C) Both thalami
D) Both hippocampi
E) Both cingulate gyri
Answer: A
Explanation: The anterior commissure connects the olfactory structures and the middle and inferior temporal gyri. The hippocampal (fornical) commissure connects the two hippocampi, while the corpus callosum connects corresponding neocortical areas.

Q: Which part of the corpus callosum, when infarcted along with the left visual cortex, is responsible for alexia without agraphia?
A) Body
B) Rostrum
C) Splenium
D) Genu
E) Anterior commissure
Answer: C
Explanation: The splenium (perfused by the posterior cerebral artery) carries visual information from the right visual cortex to the left-hemisphere language areas. Its interruption with a left occipital infarct yields pure alexia without agraphia.

Q: A 45-year-old woman presents with a mass that compresses the Broca speech area. Which gyrus and Brodmann areas should the examiner localize the lesion to?
A) Angular gyrus, area 39
B) Supramarginal gyrus, area 40
C) Middle frontal gyrus, area 8

Explanation: Broca area lies in the pars triangularis and pars opercularis of the dominant inferior frontal gyrus (areas 44 and 45). Area 22 is Wernicke, area 8 is the frontal eye field, and areas 39 and 40 are the angular and supramarginal gyri.

Q: Which gyri contain the primary auditory cortex?

Answer: E
Explanation: The transverse temporal gyri of Heschl lie within the lateral sulcus on the superior temporal gyrus and contain primary auditory areas 41 and 42.

Q: A 60-year-old man with chronic alcoholism develops hemorrhagic lesions in the mammillary bodies. To which brain division do the mammillary bodies belong?

Q: A 70-year-old woman has a lesion in the genu of the internal capsule. Which fibers are most likely interrupted?

B) Auditory radiations

Explanation: The genu contains corticobulbar fibers, which supply cranial nerve motor nuclei. Damage causes contralateral lower facial weakness and tongue deviation, among other deficits.

Q: A 19-year-old is struck on the side of the head by a baseball and briefly loses consciousness. He then wakes and seems fine for a few hours before becoming drowsy with a fixed dilated pupil. CT shows a biconvex hyperdensity under a temporal bone fracture. Which vessel was most likely torn?

Q: A 76-year-old man on warfarin develops confusion and headache 3 weeks after a minor fall. CT shows a crescent-shaped collection over the cerebral convexity that does not cross the falx attachment. Which vessel is the likely source?

Explanation: Subdural hematoma results from tearing of bridging veins that cross the subdural space to the superior sagittal sinus. It does not cross dural attachments (e.g., falx) but can cross sutures.

Q: A lumbar puncture is performed in an adult at the L3–L4 interspace. Which structure is the needle least likely to injure?

A) L3
B) S2
C) S4
D) T12
E) L1
Answer: A
Explanation: At birth the conus ends at L3; by adulthood, because of disparate growth of the spine and cord, it ends at L1–L2.

Q: Which statement about CSF is correct?
A) It leaves the fourth ventricle through a single foramen of Monro
B) The total CSF volume is about 500 mL
C) Its protein concentration is normally about 200 mg/dL in the lumbar cistern
D) Its glucose concentration is equal to plasma
E) It is produced by the choroid plexus at about 500–700 mL/day and absorbed mainly via arachnoid granulations

Explanation: CSF volume is about 140 mL, glucose about 66% of plasma, and protein under 45 mg/dL. The fourth ventricle drains through the median foramen of Magendie and two lateral foramina of Luschka.

Q: A 72-year-old woman presents with progressive dementia, a wide-based ataxic gait, and urinary incontinence. CT shows enlarged ventricles. Lumbar puncture shows normal opening pressure. Which is the best explanation for her condition?
A) Blockage of the foramen of Monro
B) Impaired CSF absorption at the arachnoid villi without elevated intracranial pressure
C) Excess loss of brain tissue with raised pressure
D) Choroid plexus papilloma
E) Aqueductal stenosis
Answer: B
Explanation: This is normal pressure hydrocephalus ("wacky, wobbly, and wet"). CSF is not absorbed by arachnoid villi, possibly after meningeal hemorrhage, and ICP is not increased. Hydrocephalus ex vacuo reflects parenchymal loss.

Q: An obese 28-year-old woman has daily headaches, bilateral papilledema, and visual deterioration. CT is normal, and CSF opening pressure is elevated. Which mechanism is implicated?
A) Loss of parenchymal volume
B) Adhesions in the subarachnoid space after meningitis
C) Obstruction of the cerebral aqueduct
D) Overproduction of CSF by a papilloma
E) Increased resistance to CSF outflow at the arachnoid villi
Answer: E
Explanation: Pseudotumor cerebri (idiopathic intracranial hypertension) typically occurs in obese women of childbearing age and is characterized by papilledema, elevated CSF pressure, and deteriorating vision.

Q: A 3-month-old has a rapidly enlarging head. MRI shows dilated lateral and third ventricles with a normal-sized fourth ventricle. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A) Aqueductal stenosis
B) Dandy-Walker malformation
C) Hydranencephaly
D) Communicating hydrocephalus after meningitis
E) Normal pressure hydrocephalus
Answer: A
Explanation: Dilation of the lateral and third ventricles with a normal fourth ventricle localizes the block to the cerebral aqueduct. It is the commonest cause of congenital hydrocephalus (obstructive/noncommunicating).

Q: A 4-year-old with fever, headache, and nuchal rigidity has CSF with >1,000 polymorphonuclear leukocytes/mm3, elevated protein, and low glucose. Which is the most likely diagnosis?
A) Subarachnoid hemorrhage
B) Guillain-Barré syndrome
C) Viral meningitis
D) Bacterial meningitis
E) Normal pressure hydrocephalus
Answer: D
Explanation: Bacterial meningitis shows a cloudy CSF with numerous neutrophils, high protein, and reduced glucose. Viral meningitis shows lymphocytes with normal glucose, and SAH shows bloody CSF with normal glucose.

Q: Which CSF profile is most consistent with viral (aseptic) meningitis?

C) Bloody, with red blood cells and normal glucose

Explanation: Viral meningitis, the most common type, shows 25–500 lymphocytes/mm3 with normal glucose.

Q: A 55-year-old with a brain tumor in the right cerebral hemisphere develops a dilated right pupil, contralateral homonymous hemianopia, and left hemiparesis. Which pair of events best explains the pupillary and visual findings?
A) Subfalcial herniation compressing the anterior cerebral artery
B) Compression of the optic chiasm and left oculomotor nerve

Explanation: Uncal (transtentorial) herniation stretches the ipsilateral CN III (dilated pupil first, as parasympathetic fibers are peripheral) and compresses the ipsilateral PCA, causing contralateral homonymous hemianopia. Pressure of the contralateral crus on the tentorial edge (Kernohan notch) may produce ipsilateral hemiparesis.

Q: Duret hemorrhages in the midbrain and rostral pons occur secondary to which mechanism?

Q: A pineal tumor compresses the dorsal midbrain. Which feature of the pineal body explains its radiologic appearance in older adults?

B) Dense bone formation from the sphenoid
C) Large neuromelanin deposits

Answer: D
Explanation: The pineal contains calcareous granules (corpora arenacea, acervulus cerebri), visible on X-ray and CT after age 16. It synthesizes melatonin and serotonin and is innervated by postganglionic fibers from the superior cervical ganglion.

Q: A 66-year-old woman has a slow-growing, well-demarcated parasagittal dural-based mass showing whorled cells and psammoma bodies. Which tumor is this?

Explanation: Meningiomas arise from arachnoid cap cells, are the most common primary CNS tumor, are more frequent in women and older adults, and show whorls and psammoma bodies. Parasagittal lesions may cause leg weakness and urinary incontinence.

Q: Where is most CSF absorbed into the venous system?

Q: A 62-year-old man undergoes repair of an abdominal aortic aneurysm. Postoperatively he has bilateral leg paralysis and loss of pain and temperature sensation below the nipple line, with preserved vibration and joint position sense. Which vessel was most likely compromised?

Explanation: The anterior spinal artery supplies the anterior two-thirds of the cord, including the corticospinal and spinothalamic tracts, and spares the posterior columns. At thoracolumbar levels the main feeder is the artery of Adamkiewicz (origin T12–L4, usually on the left).

Q: Occlusion of the posterior spinal arteries would produce which deficit?

Q: A patient reports sudden painless loss of vision in one eye. Fundoscopy shows a pale retina with a cherry-red macula. Which artery is occluded, and why is the outcome so severe?

Q: A 50-year-old woman presents with sudden severe headache, a drooping right eyelid, and a dilated right pupil with the eye turned down and out. Which vessel is most likely the site of the aneurysm?

D) Anterior communicating artery
E) Posterior communicating artery
Answer: E
Explanation: A posterior communicating artery aneurysm compresses CN III, affecting superficial parasympathetic fibers first (dilated pupil) and then the somatic fibers. Berry aneurysm rupture is a common cause of nontraumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage.

Q: Which is the commonest site of berry aneurysm in the cerebral arterial circle?

Explanation: The anterior communicating artery is the commonest site. A large aneurysm there may press on the chiasm, causing a bitemporal lower quadrantanopia.

Q: Which structure lies within the cavernous sinus itself rather than in its lateral wall?

Q: A 35-year-old woman has a left subclavian artery occlusion proximal to the vertebral artery. When she exercises her left arm she gets arm claudication and dizziness. What explains the dizziness?

Q: Which vessel passes through the foramen spinosum?

Q: Which of the following structures does NOT derive from neural crest cells?

Q: A newborn has a lumbosacral sac containing meninges and spinal cord tissue. Which condition is most likely?

Q: A child has hindbrain herniation of the cerebellar tonsils and medulla through the foramen magnum with obstructive hydrocephalus and a lumbar meningomyelocele. What is the diagnosis?

E) Hydranencephaly

Explanation: Chiari malformation is a cerebellomedullary malformation where caudal vermis, tonsils, and medulla herniate through the foramen magnum. It is frequently associated with meningomyelocele and syringomyelia.

Q: A neonate has a huge posterior fossa cyst, enlarged fourth ventricle, an elevated torcula, and agenesis of the cerebellar vermis. Which diagnosis fits best?

Explanation: Dandy–Walker results from atresia of the outlets of the fourth ventricle (foramina of Luschka and Magendie), leading to cystic dilation, vermis agenesis, and an elevated confluence of sinuses.

Q: An infant has a single ventricular cavity, absent olfactory bulbs, and midline facial anomalies. The mother drank heavily in early pregnancy. What is the most likely malformation?

Q: The sulcus limitans separates which two structures in the developing spinal cord?

Q: A 55-year-old woman has ipsilateral anosmia, ipsilateral optic atrophy, and contralateral papilledema. Which tumor is the most likely cause?

Q: After herpes simplex encephalitis, a patient is placid, hypersexual, hyperphagic, and tries to put everything in his mouth, and he does not recognize familiar objects. What is the structure most damaged?

Q: Which pathway accurately describes the circuit of Papez?

Q: Which statement about cerebellar cortex circuitry is correct?

B) Granule cells are inhibitory

Explanation: Mossy fibers (spinocerebellar, pontocerebellar) excite granule cells (glutamate), whose parallel fibers excite Purkinje cells; climbing fibers (olivocerebellar, aspartate) excite Purkinje cells directly. Basket and stellate cells inhibit Purkinje cells.
