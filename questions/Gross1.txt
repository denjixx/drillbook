Q: A 58-year-old man develops a painful vesicular rash confined to the T5 dermatome (herpes zoster). The virus lies dormant in the cell bodies of sensory neurons. In which of the following structures are the cell bodies of these unipolar (pseudounipolar) neurons located?
A) Anterior horn of the spinal cord
B) Lateral horn of the spinal cord
C) Sympathetic chain ganglion
D) Dorsal root ganglion
E) Posterior horn of the spinal cord
Answer: D
Explanation: Unipolar (pseudounipolar) neurons are the sensory neurons of the PNS and are found in the dorsal root ganglia and cranial nerve ganglia. The anterior horn contains multipolar somatic motor neurons, the lateral horn (T1–L2) contains preganglionic sympathetic neurons, sympathetic chain ganglia contain multipolar postganglionic neurons, and the posterior horn contains interneurons on which the central processes of sensory neurons synapse.

Q: A 26-year-old motorcyclist has a spinal cord lesion that selectively destroys the lateral horn between the T1 and L2 segments. Which of the following neuron populations is most likely lost?
A) Preganglionic parasympathetic neurons
B) Postganglionic sympathetic neurons
C) General somatic efferent neurons
D) Pseudounipolar sensory neurons
E) Preganglionic sympathetic neurons
Answer: E
Explanation: The lateral horn of the T1–L2 spinal cord segments contains the cell bodies of preganglionic sympathetic (general visceral efferent) neurons. Postganglionic sympathetic cell bodies lie in the sympathetic chain or prevertebral ganglia, preganglionic parasympathetic cell bodies lie in brainstem nuclei and S2–S4, somatic motor neurons lie in the anterior horn, and pseudounipolar neurons lie in the dorsal root ganglia.

Q: A 30-year-old woman with multiple sclerosis has patchy demyelination of axons in the brain and spinal cord. Which of the following cells forms the myelin that is being destroyed?
A) Schwann cell
B) Astrocyte
C) Microglial cell

E) Satellite cell
Answer: D
Explanation: Myelin in the CNS is formed by oligodendrocytes, and one oligodendrocyte can myelinate several axons. Schwann cells form myelin in the PNS, satellite cells support ganglion cell bodies, astrocytes are supportive glia, and microglia phagocytose waste products of nerve tissue.

Q: During thoracic surgery, a surgeon injures the thoracic duct, producing a milky pleural effusion. Lymph from which of the following regions would still drain normally through a different channel?

Q: A 62-year-old woman with osteoarthritis has pain at the base of her thumb and difficulty with opposition. Which type of synovial joint is the first carpometacarpal joint?
A) Hinge joint
B) Pivot joint
C) Saddle joint
D) Plane joint
E) Ball-and-socket joint
Answer: C
Explanation: The first carpometacarpal joint is a saddle joint, which permits flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and circumduction, and is the basis for opposition of the thumb. Q: A 45-year-old man with chronic pancreatitis develops steatorrhea and later diabetes mellitus. Which feature of the pancreas explains why it can produce both digestive enzymes and hormones?

B) It gives off the superior thyroid artery
C) It gives off the lingual artery
D) It gives off the facial artery
E) It gives off the ascending pharyngeal artery
Answer: A
Explanation: The internal carotid artery has no branches in the neck and enters the skull through the carotid canal, whereas the external carotid artery gives off the superior thyroid, ascending pharyngeal, lingual, facial, occipital, posterior auricular, maxillary, and superficial temporal arteries.

Q: A professional singer notices voice fatigue and inability to reach high notes after a thyroidectomy in which the superior thyroid artery was ligated near the gland. Which nerve was injured?
A) Internal laryngeal nerve
B) Recurrent laryngeal nerve
C) Hypoglossal nerve
D) External laryngeal nerve
E) Glossopharyngeal nerve
Answer: D
Explanation: The external laryngeal nerve runs with the superior thyroid artery and supplies the cricothyroid muscle, which tenses the vocal folds for high-pitched sounds. The internal laryngeal nerve is sensory above the vocal folds, and the recurrent laryngeal nerve supplies the other intrinsic muscles.

Q: A 45-year-old woman develops tingling around the mouth and carpopedal spasm after thyroidectomy. Which artery usually supplies the parathyroid glands?
A) Superior thyroid artery
B) Inferior thyroid artery
C) Thyroid ima artery
D) Ascending pharyngeal artery
E) Lingual artery
Answer: B
Explanation: The parathyroid glands, on the posterior surface of the thyroid, are supplied mainly by the inferior thyroid artery (from the thyrocervical trunk); their removal or devascularization causes hypocalcemia and tetany.

Q: A 6-year-old boy has a midline neck mass that moves upward when he protrudes his tongue. Which embryologic structure is the likely origin?
A) Second pharyngeal cleft
B) Third pharyngeal pouch
C) Persistent cervical sinus
D) Thyroglossal duct remnant
E) Lymph node
Answer: D
Explanation: The thyroid gland develops from the floor of the pharynx at the foramen cecum and descends in the neck through the thyroglossal duct, which normally disappears. A persistent remnant forms a midline cyst that moves with swallowing and protrusion of the tongue.

Q: A newborn has hypocalcemic tetany and recurrent infections due to absence of the thymus and parathyroid glands. Which embryonic structures failed to develop normally?
A) First and second pharyngeal pouches
B) Third and fourth pharyngeal pouches
C) First pharyngeal arch only
D) Second pharyngeal cleft
E) Fifth pharyngeal pouch only
Answer: B
Explanation: The third pouch forms the thymus and inferior parathyroid glands, and the fourth pouch forms the superior parathyroid glands and ultimobranchial body. Defective development produces absent parathyroids and thymus with hypocalcemia and immunodeficiency.

Q: A 9-year-old boy has a retropharyngeal abscess. Into which region can infection spread by way of this space?
A) Anterior mediastinum
B) Posterior mediastinum
C) Pleural cavity directly
D) Pericardial cavity
E) Subdural space
Answer: B
Explanation: The retropharyngeal space lies between the buccopharyngeal and prevertebral fasciae and continues inferiorly into the posterior mediastinum, so infection can descend into the chest.

Q: A surgeon identifies the branches of the thyrocervical trunk. Which artery arises from it?
A) Vertebral artery
B) Internal thoracic artery
C) Costocervical trunk
D) Suprascapular artery
E) Dorsal scapular artery in all cases
Answer: D
Explanation: The thyrocervical trunk arises from the first part of the subclavian artery and gives off the inferior thyroid, suprascapular, and transverse cervical arteries. The vertebral and internal thoracic arteries arise separately from the first part, and the costocervical trunk from the second part.

Q: During an interscalene brachial plexus block, the physician is concerned about transient diaphragmatic paralysis. Which nerve lies on the anterior surface of the scalenus anterior muscle?

B) Vagus nerve

Answer: D
Explanation: The phrenic nerve descends on the anterior surface of the scalenus anterior beneath the prevertebral fascia and may be anesthetized by interscalene block. The brachial plexus roots and trunks pass between the anterior and middle scalenes.

Q: A 40-year-old man has a parotid tumor excised. Afterward, the corner of his mouth droops on one side and the lower lip does not move normally. Which branch of the facial nerve was injured?
A) Temporal branch
B) Marginal mandibular branch
C) Zygomatic branch
D) Buccal branch
E) Cervical branch
Answer: B
Explanation: The marginal mandibular branch supplies the muscles of the lower lip and chin (depressor anguli oris and others). The facial nerve branches within the parotid gland are temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, and cervical.

Q: A 35-year-old woman wakes with inability to close her right eye, wrinkle her forehead, or smile on the right, and has reduced taste on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue. Which finding distinguishes this lesion from a cerebral stroke?
A) Paralysis of the forehead muscles
B) Loss of taste on the anterior tongue
C) Drooping of the mouth
D) Inability to smile
E) Loss of facial expression below the eye
Answer: A
Explanation: A lower motor neuron lesion of the facial nerve (Bell palsy) paralyzes the whole ipsilateral face including the forehead, because the nucleus is destroyed. In an upper motor neuron lesion (stroke), the forehead is spared since it has bilateral cortical innervation.

Q: A 12-year-old boy has painful swelling of the parotid gland (mumps). Which nerve carries the preganglionic secretomotor fibers from the glossopharyngeal nerve to the otic ganglion?
A) Greater petrosal nerve
B) Chorda tympani
C) Deep petrosal nerve
D) Lesser petrosal nerve
E) Auriculotemporal nerve
Answer: D
Explanation: The glossopharyngeal nerve gives rise to the tympanic nerve, which becomes the lesser petrosal nerve and synapses in the otic ganglion; postganglionic fibers travel with the auriculotemporal nerve to the parotid gland. The greater petrosal nerve carries facial parasympathetic fibers to the pterygopalatine ganglion.

Q: A 70-year-old woman with osteoporosis has an exaggerated thoracic curvature. Which pair of vertebral column curvatures is primary (present at birth) and concave anteriorly?
A) Cervical and lumbar curvatures
B) Thoracic and sacral curvatures
C) Cervical and thoracic curvatures
D) Lumbar and sacral curvatures
E) Cervical and sacral curvatures
Answer: B
Explanation: The thoracic and sacral curvatures are primary curvatures (kyphotic, concave anteriorly) present in the fetus. The cervical and lumbar curvatures are secondary (lordotic) curvatures that develop after birth as the infant lifts the head and begins to stand. Exaggerated thoracic curvature is kyphosis.

Q: A 55-year-old man has an infected pimple on the scalp and later develops signs of meningitis. Through which scalp layer does infection spread most readily, entering the cranial cavity through emissary veins?
A) Skin
B) Dense connective tissue
C) Epicranial aponeurosis
D) Loose areolar connective tissue
E) Pericranium
Answer: D
Explanation: The layers of the scalp are skin, connective tissue, aponeurosis (galea), loose areolar tissue, and pericranium (SCALP). The loose areolar layer is the "danger area" because infection spreads easily through it and through valveless emissary veins into the dural venous sinuses.

Q: A 20-year-old man squeezes a pustule on his upper lip and develops fever, proptosis, and ophthalmoplegia due to cavernous sinus thrombosis. Which route did the infection most likely take?
A) Facial vein to retromandibular vein to external jugular vein
B) Maxillary vein to pterygoid venous plexus to internal jugular vein
C) Facial vein to internal jugular vein
D) Superficial temporal vein to transverse sinus
E) Facial vein to angular vein to superior ophthalmic vein
Answer: E
Explanation: The facial vein communicates with the cavernous sinus through the angular vein and the superior ophthalmic vein, and these veins have no valves, so infections of the "danger triangle" of the face may spread intracranially.

Q: A neurologist tests sensation over the lower jaw and weakness of mastication. Through which foramen does the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve leave the skull?
A) Foramen rotundum
B) Foramen spinosum
C) Superior orbital fissure
D) Foramen lacerum
E) Foramen ovale
Answer: E
Explanation: The mandibular nerve (V3) passes through the foramen ovale, the maxillary nerve (V2) through the foramen rotundum, and the ophthalmic nerve (V1) through the superior orbital fissure. The foramen spinosum transmits the middle meningeal artery.

Q: A 22-year-old man has a lucid interval after a blow to the temple, then deteriorates. CT shows a lens-shaped epidural hematoma beneath a fracture of the pterion. Which vessel was torn?
A) Superficial temporal artery
B) Bridging cerebral vein
C) Middle meningeal artery
D) Superior sagittal sinus
E) Middle cerebral artery
Answer: C
Explanation: The pterion is the thin junction of the frontal, parietal, temporal, and sphenoid bones overlying the anterior branch of the middle meningeal artery, which arises from the maxillary artery and enters through the foramen spinosum. Its rupture produces an epidural hematoma.

Q: An 80-year-old alcoholic man has progressive headache and confusion after a minor fall. CT shows a crescent-shaped collection between the dura and arachnoid. Which vessels are most likely torn?
A) Middle meningeal artery
B) Bridging (superior cerebral) veins
C) Anterior cerebral artery
D) Vertebral artery
E) Posterior communicating artery
Answer: B
Explanation: Subdural hematoma results from tearing of the bridging veins that cross the subdural space from the cerebral cortex to the superior sagittal sinus, particularly in elderly persons with brain atrophy. Epidural hematomas are usually arterial.

Q: A patient with cavernous sinus thrombosis cannot abduct the right eye. Which nerve is most vulnerable because it runs within the sinus next to the internal carotid artery rather than in its lateral wall?
A) Oculomotor nerve
B) Abducens nerve
C) Trochlear nerve
D) Ophthalmic nerve
E) Maxillary nerve
Answer: B
Explanation: The abducens nerve passes through the cavernous sinus lumen lateral to the internal carotid artery, whereas the oculomotor, trochlear, ophthalmic, and maxillary nerves lie in the lateral wall. Abducens paralysis causes medial deviation of the eye.

Q: A 45-year-old woman has a sudden severe headache, a dilated pupil, and a drooping eyelid with the eye turned down and out. Which aneurysm is the most likely cause?
A) Posterior communicating artery aneurysm compressing the oculomotor nerve
B) Anterior communicating artery aneurysm compressing the optic chiasm
C) Basilar tip aneurysm compressing the trochlear nerve
D) Middle cerebral artery aneurysm compressing the optic nerve
E) Vertebral artery aneurysm compressing the hypoglossal nerve
Answer: A
Explanation: The oculomotor nerve runs close to the posterior communicating artery as it passes from the midbrain; an aneurysm compresses its parasympathetic fibers (dilated pupil) and motor fibers, causing ptosis and a "down and out" eye.

Q: A child has hydrocephalus due to obstruction of arachnoid villi. Through which structures does cerebrospinal fluid normally enter the venous circulation?
A) Choroid plexus into the vein of Galen
B) Cerebral aqueduct into the sigmoid sinus
C) Arachnoid granulations into the superior sagittal sinus
D) Foramen of Monro into the cavernous sinus
E) Central canal into the internal jugular vein
Answer: C
Explanation: CSF, produced by the choroid plexuses, flows from the lateral ventricles through the interventricular foramen into the third ventricle, through the cerebral aqueduct to the fourth ventricle, out through its apertures to the subarachnoid space, and is absorbed by arachnoid granulations into the superior sagittal sinus.

Q: A skull base fracture damages structures passing through the jugular foramen. Which cranial nerves are at risk?
A) Facial, vestibulocochlear, and glossopharyngeal nerves
B) Glossopharyngeal, vagus, and accessory nerves
C) Oculomotor, trochlear, and abducens nerves
D) Hypoglossal and accessory nerves
E) Trigeminal, facial, and vagus nerves
Answer: B
Explanation: The jugular foramen transmits the glossopharyngeal, vagus, and accessory nerves and the internal jugular vein. The facial and vestibulocochlear nerves enter the internal acoustic meatus, and the hypoglossal nerve passes through the hypoglossal canal.

Q: A 30-year-old man has a fracture of the cribriform plate after a fall. He has clear fluid dripping from his nose and cannot smell. Which structures are injured?
A) Optic nerve and ophthalmic artery
B) Facial nerve and chorda tympani
C) Olfactory nerve fibers and meninges
D) Maxillary nerve and middle meningeal artery
E) Abducens nerve and internal carotid artery
Answer: C
Explanation: The cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone transmits the olfactory nerve filaments from the nasal mucosa to the olfactory bulb in the anterior cranial fossa; fracture causes anosmia and cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhea when the meninges tear.

Q: Which structure traverses the optic canal together with the optic nerve?
A) Superior ophthalmic vein
B) Abducens nerve
C) Ophthalmic artery
D) Oculomotor nerve
E) Lacrimal nerve
Answer: C
Explanation: The optic canal transmits the optic nerve and the ophthalmic artery. The superior orbital fissure transmits the oculomotor, trochlear, abducens, and ophthalmic nerve branches (lacrimal, frontal, nasociliary) and the ophthalmic veins.

Q: A patient with head trauma has diplopia on looking downward when the eye is adducted, and he tilts his head to compensate. Which muscle is paralyzed?
A) Superior oblique
B) Inferior oblique
C) Lateral rectus
D) Medial rectus
E) Superior rectus
Answer: A
Explanation: The trochlear nerve (CN IV) supplies the superior oblique muscle, which depresses the adducted eye (as in reading or descending stairs) and intorts it. The lateral rectus is supplied by the abducens nerve, and the other extraocular muscles by the oculomotor nerve.

Q: In a patient with complete oculomotor nerve palsy, which extraocular muscle will still function?
A) Lateral rectus
B) Medial rectus
C) Inferior rectus
D) Inferior oblique
E) Levator palpebrae superioris
Answer: A
Explanation: The lateral rectus (CN VI) and superior oblique (CN IV) are spared in oculomotor palsy, so the eye is abducted and slightly depressed ("down and out"). The medial, superior, and inferior recti, inferior oblique, and levator palpebrae superioris are paralyzed.

Q: A 60-year-old smoker has an apical lung tumor with ptosis, miosis, and facial anhidrosis. Which muscle is responsible for the ptosis in Horner syndrome?
A) Levator palpebrae superioris
B) Superior tarsal muscle
C) Orbicularis oculi
D) Frontalis
E) Dilator pupillae
Answer: B
Explanation: Interruption of the cervical sympathetic pathway paralyzes the superior tarsal (Müller) muscle, which elevates the upper eyelid slightly, causing mild ptosis; paralysis of the dilator pupillae causes miosis. The levator palpebrae superioris is supplied by the oculomotor nerve.

Q: Shining a light into one eye causes constriction of both pupils. Which structures carry the efferent limb of this reflex?

Answer: C
Explanation: The afferent limb is the optic nerve; the efferent limb is the parasympathetic fibers from the Edinger-Westphal nucleus through the oculomotor nerve to the ciliary ganglion and short ciliary nerves to the sphincter pupillae.

Q: A child has epiphora (excessive tearing) due to blockage of the nasolacrimal duct. Where does this duct drain into the nasal cavity?

Explanation: The nasolacrimal duct drains tears from the lacrimal sac into the inferior nasal meatus, below the inferior concha. The maxillary sinus and frontal sinus drain into the middle meatus.

Q: A patient with a lesion of the greater petrosal nerve has dry eyes. In which ganglion do the preganglionic fibers for the lacrimal gland synapse?

Q: A 65-year-old woman has acute angle-closure glaucoma. Aqueous humor normally leaves the anterior chamber through which structure?

Explanation: Aqueous humor is produced by the ciliary processes, flows from the posterior to the anterior chamber, and drains through the trabecular meshwork into the scleral venous sinus. Blockage increases intraocular pressure and damages the optic nerve.

Q: A 45-year-old woman begins to need reading glasses. During accommodation for near vision, which of the following changes normally occurs?

Explanation: For near vision, parasympathetic fibers (oculomotor nerve through the ciliary ganglion) contract the ciliary muscle, which relaxes tension on the suspensory ligament and allows the elastic lens to become thicker and more convex, along with pupillary constriction and convergence.

Q: A 25-year-old man has a dislocated temporomandibular joint after yawning. Which muscle is the principal one that depresses and protrudes the mandible to open the mouth?

Explanation: The lateral pterygoid pulls the condyle and articular disc forward and opens the mouth (with the suprahyoid muscles); all other muscles of mastication elevate the mandible. All are innervated by the mandibular nerve.

Q: A dentist anesthetizes the inferior alveolar nerve. Which nerve joining the lingual nerve carries taste from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue and preganglionic parasympathetic fibers to the submandibular ganglion?

Q: A 55-year-old man has a lesion of the hypoglossal nerve. Which tongue muscle will still function because it is not innervated by this nerve?

Q: A 50-year-old man has a stone in the submandibular duct and undergoes surgery. Which nerve crosses the duct as it passes beneath it and is at risk?

Explanation: In the floor of the mouth, the lingual nerve loops under the submandibular duct from lateral to medial, so it may be injured in surgery on the duct or gland. The hypoglossal nerve lies lateral and deep to the hyoglossus muscle.

Q: A 60-year-old man has a lesion of the right vagus nerve. When he says "ah," in which direction does the uvula deviate?

Explanation: The vagus supplies the levator veli palatini and uvular muscles (via the pharyngeal plexus); the uvula is pulled toward the normal side, away from the lesion. The tensor veli palatini is supplied by the mandibular nerve.

Q: A newborn has a cleft in the hard and soft palates posterior to the incisive foramen. Failure of which structures to fuse is responsible?

Q: A neurologist tests the gag reflex. Which nerve carries the afferent limb?

Explanation: The afferent limb of the gag reflex is the glossopharyngeal nerve (sensation from the pharyngeal wall), and the efferent limb is the vagus nerve (pharyngeal muscle contraction).

Q: A 40-year-old man has a lesion of the glossopharyngeal nerve. Which pharyngeal muscle is most likely paralyzed?

Q: A patient with bilateral paralysis of one intrinsic laryngeal muscle has stridor because the vocal folds cannot open. Which muscle is the only abductor of the vocal folds?
